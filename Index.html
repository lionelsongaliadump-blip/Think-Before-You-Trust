A student guide to responsible AI use

Use AI to learn.
Don't outsource your thinking.

AI can help you brainstorm, understand difficult ideas, and organize your work. But a fluent answer is not automatically a correct one. This guide shows how to use AI as a learning aid while keeping your own judgment at the center.

1. What is AI useful for?

Think of generative AI as an assistant for exploring ideas—not as an unquestionable authority or a replacement for your learning.

  • Understand concepts: Ask for a simpler explanation, an analogy, or a step-by-step example.
  • Brainstorm: Generate possible topics, research questions, outlines, or counterarguments.
  • Practise: Request practice questions, flashcards, or feedback on your reasoning.
  • Improve a draft: Ask for feedback on clarity and organization, then decide which suggestions make sense.
  • Plan research: Use AI to suggest keywords and search strategies, then find and read the actual sources yourself.
Keep ownership of your work. You should be able to explain, defend, and take responsibility for the ideas you submit.

2. Know the limits of AI

AI tools can produce answers that sound confident while containing mistakes. They may omit context, reflect bias, provide outdated information, misinterpret a question, or invent details and references.

  • Fluent does not mean factual. Polished writing is not evidence that a statement is true.
  • AI may miss context. It might misunderstand your instructions or leave out an important perspective.
  • Answers can be outdated. Current laws, statistics, events, and scientific developments need up-to-date sources.
  • AI can agree with a mistaken assumption. Ask what evidence supports a claim and consider alternative explanations.
Remember: Asking the same AI tool to confirm its own answer is not independent verification. Look for evidence outside that answer.

3. How to check AI-generated information

Use this simple process when an AI response includes factual claims that matter to your assignment.

1

Identify the claim

Pick out the key fact, statistic, quotation, date, or explanation you may rely on. Break a long answer into separate claims.

2

Find an independent source

Search for the topic yourself. Prefer original documents, official data, scholarly publications, textbooks, or reputable reporting suited to the subject.

3

Read the source

Don't rely only on a search snippet or an AI summary. Open the source and check whether it actually supports the claim.

4

Compare and decide

Check important claims against another reliable source when appropriate. Correct, qualify, or leave out claims that remain unsupported.

What makes a source worth using?

  • Authority: Who wrote or published it? Are they qualified or responsible for the information?
  • Evidence: Does it show data, references, documents, or a clear basis for its claims?
  • Currency: Is it recent enough for your topic?
  • Relevance: Does it answer the question you are researching?
  • Purpose: Is it informing, persuading, selling, or advocating for something?

4. Check AI-generated citations and quotations

A reference can look academic and still be inaccurate or nonexistent. Even when a real publication exists, the AI may give the wrong author, title, date, journal, link, or summary.

  1. Search the exact article or book title in a search engine, library catalogue, or scholarly database.
  2. Confirm that the author, title, publication, date, and DOI or URL match the actual source.
  3. Open the source and read the relevant section.
  4. Check that the source supports the statement you want to use—not merely that it discusses a similar topic.
  5. If you cannot locate or verify the reference, do not present it as a confirmed source. Find another source instead.
Important: Cite the original source you actually consulted, following your teacher's required citation style. Do not treat an AI-generated bibliography as automatically verified.

5. Ask better questions

Clear prompts can make AI more useful, but they cannot guarantee that its answers are correct.

Try including these details

  • Task: Explain, compare, brainstorm, quiz me, or give feedback.
  • Context: State your topic and what you already understand.
  • Limits: Specify length, level, format, or what the answer should avoid.
  • Learning goal: Ask for an explanation or hints that help you understand, not only a finished answer.
Example prompt: learning a difficult concept

“Explain this concept at a senior high school level. Give one example, point out common misunderstandings, and then ask me three questions to check my understanding.”

Example prompt: checking a factual claim

“List the factual claims in this paragraph that need checking. For each claim, suggest search terms and the type of reliable source I should look for. Do not assume a claim is true just because it appears in this paragraph.”

Example prompt: improving a draft

“Give feedback on the clarity and organization of my draft. Explain the reasons for each suggestion, and do not rewrite the entire piece for me.”

6. Use AI with academic integrity

  • Follow your teacher's instructions and your school's rules about permitted AI use.
  • Do not submit AI-generated work as entirely your own if that misrepresents how it was produced.
  • Understand and check any information you include in your work.
  • Disclose AI assistance when your teacher or assignment requires it.
  • Do not invent sources, data, interviews, observations, or research results.
  • Use AI to support your learning—not to bypass the learning the assignment is designed to develop.

Rules differ across schools, subjects, and assignments. When unsure, ask your teacher what forms of AI assistance are allowed.

7. Protect your privacy

Before entering information into an AI tool, consider where it will go and how the service may handle it.

  • Do not enter passwords, private messages, student ID numbers, personal records, or sensitive details about yourself or others.
  • Remove identifying information from examples whenever possible.
  • Check the tool's privacy settings and terms, and follow school guidance.
  • Use only tools and accounts that meet your age requirements and your school's rules.

Your quick pre-submission checklist

Before using AI-assisted information in schoolwork, ask yourself:

  • □ Do I understand the information I am using?
  • □ Have I independently checked important facts and claims?
  • □ Have I opened and checked the sources I intend to cite?
  • □ Does each source actually support the claim I attribute to it?
  • □ Have I considered missing context, bias, or outdated information?
  • □ Am I following my teacher's rules for AI use and disclosure?
  • □ Have I protected private or identifying information?

Use AI thoughtfully.

AI can help you move forward—but your judgment, evidence, and responsibility still matter.
